If you've spent time in parenting communities, you've probably heard the debate around CoComelon. Some parents report that their children become glued to the screen, lose interest in playing, or have meltdowns when the videos are turned off.[^parents] These experiences have raised concerns about hyperstimulation, where production choices such as rapid scene changes, bright colors, and constant music may overwhelm young children's developing ability to process what they see and hear.[^forbes][^newyorker] We developed a way to measure these production choices and compare stimulation levels across children's videos.

Compare these two versions of the same nursery rhyme. Pay attention to how often the scenes change and how much motion you see.

In the first video, the scene constantly switches between different shots. The camera keeps moving around the animated characters, and even the lyrics move across the screen. In contrast, the second video uses a fixed camera throughout, with only small movements from the ducks themselves. Despite featuring the same nursery rhyme, the two videos create very different viewing experiences.

Putting these differences into numbers, the first video has an average of 32.4 cuts per minute, roughly one every two seconds, while the second has 4.9 cuts per minute, roughly one every 12 seconds. Our motion analysis also detects fast movement during 42.8% of the first video, compared with 0.2% in the second.

Creators routinely use analytics dashboards, A/B testing of thumbnails and edits, and third-party optimization services to understand what attracts more views and keeps audiences watching. If a creator notices that faster cuts, brighter visuals, or louder audio coincide with better performance, they may be encouraged to repeat those choices. Over time, these small adjustments can accumulate, gradually making videos more stimulating without creators deliberately setting out to do so. When many creators respond to similar incentives, the broader landscape of children's videos may shift toward greater stimulation.

We analyzed 16,375 videos from 542 child-facing YouTube channels, published between 2015 and 2026. We found increases in several stimulation patterns over time, with rapid cutting showing the most widespread increase. Looking within individual channels, nearly one in four showed a significant increase in at least one stimulation type, with 36 channels increasing their cutting rates.

This is why we built ***HyperStim?***. We want to make stimulation in children's videos more transparent for both parents and creators. The platform shows how videos compare with others, helping creators better understand their production choices and giving parents a way to assess stimulation levels and find videos that better match their children's needs and preferences.
